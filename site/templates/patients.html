A patient's guide to this site

Insurance companies publish lists of the doctors and therapists in each plan. Those lists are often wrong. This site checks the lists and grades them.

What the grades mean

Every plan gets a grade in every county it covers, based only on the plan's own published provider list.

  • A or B: the list is internally consistent and current. Most entries have real contact information and identifiers the federal registry recognizes.
  • C or D: a meaningful share of entries have problems: fake-looking phone numbers, years-old update dates, or identifiers the federal registry has retired.
  • F: much of the list is unusable exactly as published. In the worst files, most entries have no working contact information at all.
  • n=4: the plan lists fewer than 10 mental-health providers in that county. We do not grade a list that small. The small number is the finding.

One honest limit, stated plainly: we read files. We do not call offices. A good grade means the plan's published list is consistent and current. It does not promise that an office will answer the phone or have appointments open.

If you are choosing a plan

  1. Find your county with the search box on the home page, or through your state's page.
  2. For each plan you are considering, look at two things together: the mental-health grade and the provider count. A plan with grade B and 400 listed providers gives you far better odds than a plan with grade B and 12.
  3. Click the plan's name to see its whole footprint. A plan that grades F in every county it operates in tells you how the insurer maintains its lists everywhere.

If you already have a plan and cannot reach a therapist

You have more leverage than most people know. Use it in this order.

  1. Document every attempt. Date, provider name, number called, and what happened: disconnected, not in network, not accepting, no response. Three to five documented failures is a strong record.
  2. Ask your plan, in writing, for an in-network appointment. Call the member services number on your card, then follow up in writing. Ask them to name a mental-health provider who is in network, accepting new patients, and reachable, with an appointment within a reasonable time.
  3. If they cannot, request a network exception. Ask, in writing, for out-of-network care at in-network cost sharing because the plan has no accessible in-network provider. Plans grant these. Insurers and regulators call this a network adequacy exception, a network gap exception, or a single-case agreement. Use those words.
  4. If the plan refuses, complain to your state insurance department. Every state has one, and network adequacy is their territory. Find yours through the NAIC state map. Attach your documented attempts.
  5. Attach the evidence from this site. Your plan's page here shows what its own published list contains. The issuer's evidence file, on the data page, itemizes every problem record. A complaint that says "the plan's own federally mandated file shows placeholder phone numbers on half its entries" is hard to dismiss.

This is general information, not legal advice. Marketplace coverage questions can also go to the federal help line at HealthCare.gov.

Words this site uses

CellOne plan in one county. The unit every grade attaches to.
Attestation dateThe date a plan's file claims a provider entry was last updated. Federal rules require monthly updates.
NPIA provider's federal identifier. When the registry retires one and a plan still lists it, the entry is usually a retired, relocated, or deceased provider.
Out-of-area listingA provider attached to a plan whose service area contains none of that provider's addresses. It pads the list without adding anyone you can see.
Plan IDThe 14-character code (like 12345TX0010001) that identifies a plan in federal filings. Your plan documents carry it.
