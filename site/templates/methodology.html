How the Directory Integrity Score works

Every score is computed from the insurer's own federally mandated machine-readable directory file and dated federal registry snapshots. No other inputs are used.

What we measure, and what we do not

Federal rules (45 CFR 156.230(b), implemented through CMS's issuer letters) require every marketplace insurer to publish its provider directory as machine-readable JSON at a public URL and to update it no less than monthly. This project fetches those files every month, archives them with cryptographic hashes, and checks whether the published data is internally consistent, current, and usable. The score is not a measure of care quality, network adequacy, or any individual provider. A flag asserts what a dated file shows, never why.

The ten checks

Feed level

Dead feed. The CMS-submitted URL cannot be retrieved. Plans are graded X, unauditable: an F asserts bad content, an X asserts absent content.

Access restricted. The file is served only to web-browser User-Agents. Published as a transparency note; no score effect, because the content, once fetched, is complete.

Record level, from the file alone

Placeholder values. Phones like 999999999, zip 99999, street address "null", attestation dates before 2014. Deterministic, and the strongest flag class: a record with placeholder contact data is unusable exactly as published.

Stale attestations. Records whose own last_updated_on is more than 180 days old (heavier at 365). One caveat is mandatory. In three quarters of files, every record shares a single date, so the field behaves as a file-generation timestamp. An old date shows real staleness. A fresh date shows only that the file was regenerated. We present fresh dates as regeneration, never as verification.

Single shared phone only. Every phone on the record is a number shared with at least 50 records or 1% of the file. We attribute the number's owner where known (a plan's own line is worse than a provider organization's central booking line) and check NPPES for a diverging direct phone.

Address inflation. Individual providers listed at more than 10 unique addresses (organizations, 25), with exclusions for specialties that legitimately cover many sites. The worst observed records list one clinician at hundreds of addresses.

Out-of-area listings. Records attached to a plan with no address inside the plan's CMS-filed service area, nor any adjacent county. These records inflate apparent network size while being unable to serve the plan's geography as filed. They never enter county rosters and are scored plan-wide.

Accepting-patients field missing. Individual records leaving the CMS-required accepting field blank or unknown. Facility and group records are exempt by schema and never counted.

Record level, against the federal registry

NPI registry status. Identifiers that are malformed, fail the checksum, or appear on the NPPES Deactivated NPI Report while absent from the active registry file. NPIs are deactivated for many reasons, including retirement, death, and entity changes. The flag asserts only that a current directory entry carries an identifier the federal registry has retired, as of a dated report.

Specialty and taxonomy disagreement. The file lists the provider under a behavioral-health specialty while the registry records only unrelated taxonomy groups. One of the two databases is wrong about this provider's specialty. We do not know which one. The flag states both facts.

From flags to grades

The scoring unit is one plan in one county. A record's flags combine sublinearly, so one broken record is never counted four times. County-roster integrity carries 80% of the score; plan-file hygiene, which carries the out-of-area rate, the remaining 20%. Cross-database flags can deduct at most 20 points: registry disagreement is capped until the correction channel has operated a full cycle, because the registry itself is self-reported and lags. Bands: A 90 and up, B 80, C 70, D 55, F below 55. Bands are ordinal transparency tiers, calibrated to nothing external in this version; a recalibration against the observed distribution is planned and will be published with before and after.

Cells with 10 to 29 roster records carry a low-sample badge. Cells with fewer than 10 behavioral-health records are not graded at all; the roster count itself is published as the finding.

Evidence strength, on every flag

E1 reproducible from the issuer's archived bytes alone. E2 same evidence, but through a chosen threshold; the thresholds and the flag rates at alternate thresholds are published. E3 a dated disagreement between the file and a federal registry snapshot; either database could be the stale one, and the flag always carries the registry date and the innocent-causes note.

Known biases we publish

Attestation bulk-stamping means freshness is never exculpatory. Fields that are absent on facility and group records by schema are never counted as defects. One hosting platform serves many issuers, so a platform bug correlates scores across issuers; issuer pages disclose their hosting. The registry is not ground truth, which is exactly why cross-database deductions are capped.

Process guarantees

Versioned methodology and rule versions on every evidence row. Every fetched file is archived with URL, timestamp, response headers, and SHA-256; claims cite the snapshot, not the live URL. Issuers' CMS-listed technical contacts receive their complete machine-readable flag export at least 14 days before first publication. Disputes are published verbatim next to the flags they dispute; flags cleared by a later crawl are marked resolved with a date, and history is retained. Crawls run monthly, aligned to registry releases; pages older than 60 days badge themselves as awaiting re-crawl.
